Physical Practices
Resistance Training

Strength Work

3 sessions per week · 30–45 minutes · Meal 1 (full supplement smoothie) immediately after

🕑 When: Training day mornings — resistance training begins at 5:42am, Meal 1 at ~6:30am 💪 Phases: All phases, never interrupted
Why this matters neurologically: Resistance training builds the anterior midcingulate cortex (aMCC) — the brain region governing voluntary override of involuntary responses. The aMCC strengthens specifically through aversive voluntary effort: the last reps you want to skip, the set that is genuinely uncomfortable, the moment you choose to continue. This is the same cortical region needed to override the misophonic threat response in daily life. The discomfort is not incidental to the training. It is the mechanism.
Structure: Cover all 5 movement patterns across the week using 2–3 exercises per session. 3 sets per exercise, building to 4 as strength increases. Rest 60–90 seconds between sets. The specific sequence matters less than covering all patterns. Full template at the bottom of this section.
01 Squat — Lower body ►

What it is

Bending at the hips and knees to lower the body, then standing back up. Builds the quadriceps, glutes, hamstrings, and core. The single most fundamental human movement pattern.

Beginner progression

Box squatSquat to a chair or box, barely touch it, stand. 3×8–10. Teaches depth without fear of falling. Start here.
Bodyweight squatOnce box squat is comfortable. Arms forward for balance. 3×10–12.
Goblet squatHold one weight at chest (dumbbell, water jug, bag of rice). The weight counterbalances and helps you stay upright. 3×8–10.

Form — what to watch

  • Feet shoulder-width apart, toes pointed slightly outward (15–30°)
  • Push the knees outward — in the same direction as the toes
  • Chest stays up — do not let it collapse forward
  • Lower until thighs approach parallel to the floor, or as far as comfortable
  • Drive through the whole foot to stand — not just the toes
  • If heels rise off the floor: widen stance or place a thin plate under heels

Progress when

3 sets × 12 feels easy with clean form. Add load, or move to the next progression.

02 Hip Hinge — Posterior chain ►

What it is

Pushing the hips backward and hinging the torso forward while keeping the back flat, then driving the hips forward to return upright. Builds the glutes, hamstrings, and lower back. The foundation of all pulling from the floor.

Learn the pattern first

Stand one foot from a wall. Keep a slight bend in the knees. Push your hips straight back until they touch the wall — do not squat down, just push back. Feel how the hamstrings load. That tension is the hip hinge. Now drive the hips forward to return. That is the full movement.

Hip hinge to wallLearn the pattern as above, 3×10 reps, no weight. Master this before loading.
Romanian deadlift (RDL)Hold light weights at thighs, hinge back keeping the weights sliding close to the legs. Hands reach mid-shin level. 3×8–10.
DeadliftOnce RDL form is reliable. More demanding, more reward. Start very light. 3×5–6.

Form — what to watch

  • Back stays flat throughout — the moment it rounds, the weight is too heavy
  • Hinge at the hip, not the waist — the feeling is pushing the hips back, not bending forward
  • Hamstrings should feel loaded on the way down — that loading is correct
  • Drive the hips forward to come up — squeeze the glutes at the top
  • Soft bend in the knees — this is not a squat; legs mostly stay straight

Progress when

3 sets × 10 with light load feels straightforward and form is consistent. Increase load incrementally.

03 Push — Chest, shoulders, triceps ►

What it is

Moving a load (or your own body) away from you. Builds the chest, shoulders, and triceps. Comes in two directions: horizontal (pressing forward) and vertical (pressing overhead).

Wall push-upAbsolute beginner starting point. Hands on wall, lean forward and push. 3×10–15.
Incline push-upHands on a bench or low step. Harder than wall, easier than floor. 3×8–12.
Push-upFull floor push-up. Hands slightly wider than shoulders. 3×6–10, building toward 3×15.
Dumbbell overhead pressSeated or standing, press weights from shoulders to overhead. 3×8–10.

Form — push-up specifically

  • Body is one straight line from head to heels — hips must not sag downward
  • Lower chest to 1–2 inches from the floor before pressing up
  • Elbows travel at roughly 45° from the body — not flared out to 90°
  • Squeeze glutes and abdominals throughout — this protects the lower back
  • If full push-ups are too hard, return to incline until strength builds

Progress when

3 sets × 15 is achievable with clean form. Move to the next harder variation.

04 Pull — Upper back, biceps ►

What it is

Moving a weight toward you, or your body toward a fixed point. Builds the upper back, rear deltoids, and biceps. Often underdeveloped relative to pushing — critical for posture and shoulder health.

Band pull-apartResistance band held at chest height with both hands, pull apart until arms are extended to each side. 3×15. Accessible anywhere with a band.
Inverted row (table or low bar)Lie under a sturdy table, grip the edge, pull chest up to the edge. 3×8–10. An excellent bodyweight pull alternative to pull-ups.
Dumbbell rowOne knee and one hand on a bench, row the weight from hanging to hip. 3×8–10 each side.
Pull-up (if available)Full body weight from a bar. Begin with negatives: jump to the top position and lower yourself slowly. 3×3–5.

Form — dumbbell row

  • Keep the back flat and parallel to the floor — do not twist the torso to pull more weight
  • Drive the elbow toward the ceiling, not the hand toward the shoulder
  • Pause briefly at the top — feel the shoulder blade squeeze toward the spine
  • Lower slowly and under control — the downward phase builds as much strength as the pull

Progress when

3 sets × 12 with clean form. Increase weight or advance to the next exercise in the progression.

05 Carry — Full body, core, grip ►

What it is

Walking while holding a load. Trains core stability and grip strength under sustained load, builds total-body resilience, and has disproportionate benefit relative to its apparent simplicity. Beginners can use any heavy objects — bags of rice, water jugs, filled backpacks.

Farmer carry (both hands)Pick up two heavy objects (one in each hand), walk 20–30 meters at moderate pace. Tall posture, shoulders back. 3–4 sets.
Suitcase carry (one hand)One-sided. Harder on the core — resist any urge to lean toward the weight. 3 sets each side × 20m.
Rack carry / bottoms-upWeight held at shoulder height. Advanced — only when the farmer carry is solid.

Form

  • Stand as tall as possible — shoulders back, ribs down, head neutral
  • Do not lean sideways toward the weight (suitcase carry)
  • Breathe normally — do not hold the breath
  • The carry ends when form breaks, not when the timer ends

Load guidance

Use whatever challenges the grip by the end of each set. Start with whatever feels heavy but manageable for 30 meters. Progress by increasing load or distance week to week.

Zone 2 Aerobic + Cold Immersion

Zone 2 & Waterfall

2 sessions per week · Post-morning meeting · BDNF peaks ~2 hours after exercise

🕑 When: Post-meeting (~9:15am) → Meal 1 at 11:30am → CC at 12:30pm if scheduled 🏃 Venue: Waterfall preferred — any Zone 2 + cold shower works
Why this matters: Zone 2 aerobic exercise is the primary non-pharmacological BDNF upregulator available. BDNF peaks approximately 2 hours post-exercise — this is why CC sessions on aerobic days are scheduled at 12:30pm, approximately 2 hours after returning from the waterfall. The cold plunge produces a sustained norepinephrine surge of 200–300% lasting several hours and immediately activates vagal tone via the diving reflex. The waterfall run delivers BDNF elevation, bilateral sensory integration, vagal activation, cortisol rhythm anchoring, and somatic reset simultaneously — no single device in the protocol produces this breadth of effect.

How to identify Zone 2 intensity

Conversation testYou can hold a full conversation in complete sentences, but breathing is clearly elevated. You would not want to sing. If you cannot speak in sentences: too hard. If effort feels negligible: too easy.
Heart rate guideRoughly 60–70% of maximum HR. Estimate: 220 minus age. For age 35: max ~185, Zone 2 = 111–130 bpm. This is a rough guide — the feel test is more reliable.
Duration45–60 minutes at this steady pace. Continuity matters more than intensity — steady Zone 2 produces more BDNF than intervals at the same duration.
End feelPleasantly tired, not depleted. Able to hold a conversation within 5 minutes of stopping.

Waterfall Protocol

01
Post-meeting departure · ~45–60 min climb

Zone 2 climb to the waterfall

Maintain conversational pace throughout. On steeper sections where the terrain naturally forces you above Zone 2, slow down or pause briefly — consistency of Zone 2 effort matters more than covering maximum distance at high intensity.

This is the primary aerobic stimulus. Treat it seriously. Rushing the climb defeats the purpose — BDNF production from Zone 2 is duration-dependent.

02
At the waterfall · 3–5 minutes

Cold immersion

Enter slowly. Full immersion, or as deep as conditions safely allow. Target 3–5 minutes total. The first 30–60 seconds involve the cold shock response — rapid shallow breathing, heart rate spike, urge to exit. This is expected and normal. Do not fight it. Breathe slowly and deliberately. That transition from shock to calm is the nervous system shift — the vagal activation happens there.

The norepinephrine surge begins within the first 30 seconds and peaks around 3 minutes. Staying through the shock response is what produces the effect. Exiting during the shock response provides little benefit.

Safety first: If water flow is high or footing is unstable, do not enter. Take the cold shower fallback instead. Never hyperventilate before cold immersion.

03
Return walk · natural pace

Recovery walk home

Natural, relaxed pace. Allow core temperature to normalize. This is not wasted time — active recovery at low intensity sustains elevated BDNF while the nervous system integrates the cold stimulus. Arrive home in a settled, alert state.

04
11:30am

Meal 1 — First food of the day

NMN sublingual 5 minutes before eating, then full supplement stack with the protein smoothie. This is the first food after a 19h+ fast. BDNF from the waterfall is still elevated and rising toward its peak.

05
~12:30pm (CC days)

CC session — inside the BDNF window

Approximately 2 hours after returning from the waterfall, BDNF is at or near peak. This is the optimal neuroplasticity window for counterconditioning. On CC days: somatic release (full) → tDCS (Phase 3+) → CC with tVNS → EMDR/IFS if scheduled. The window is real — do not delay the CC session past 2pm on aerobic days.

Fallback when waterfall is unavailable: Any Zone 2 movement for 45–60 minutes (brisk walk, cycling, swimming) followed immediately by a cold shower finish of 30–60 seconds. The session matters more than the venue. The cold shower is a meaningful fallback — turn it to cold, stay for 30–60 seconds, breathe through the shock response.
Morning Practice

Qi-Gong

15 minutes · Every morning · Begins Phase 1 Week 4

🕑 Training days: After somatic release (~7:25am) 🕑 Aerobic days: After somatic release (~6:00am) ⚠ Illness: 3-minute shaking only — do not skip entirely
Why this matters: Overnight somatic bracing — elevated shoulders, held jaw, shallow chest breathing, contracted psoas — keeps the brainstem in low-level threat alert regardless of actual threat. This is the baseline the day begins from. The shaking practice physically breaks this overnight pattern in under 5 minutes, by a different mechanism than the somatic release floor work. The flowing practice trains the capacity to hold strong internal somatic sensation while the external presentation remains soft and controlled — exactly what counterconditioning requires from the nervous system.

Part 1 — Shaking Practice (5 minutes)

01
Setup

Standing position

Stand with feet hip-width apart. Soft bend in the knees — never locked straight. Spine relaxed, not held rigid. Arms hanging fully loose at the sides. Jaw unclenched, lips slightly parted. Eyes open or closed, whatever feels natural.

02
First 2 minutes — gentle

Begin bouncing from the knees

Small, rhythmic bouncing initiated from the knees — rising no more than 1–2 cm. Let the bounce travel upward: through the hips, belly, chest, shoulders, down the arms, and into the jaw. The key distinction: you are not doing the shaking. You are letting the bouncing shake you. Keep everything soft — soft jaw, soft belly, soft hands, soft face. Breathe naturally.

03
Minutes 2–4 — let it amplify

Allow the shake to build

The shaking may intensify on its own as the body releases held tension — allow this without controlling it. Arms may start swinging loosely. Jaw may chatter slightly. Hands may flap. This is the overnight bracing pattern being physically broken. Do not suppress or choreograph it. If it feels strange or slightly involuntary, that is correct.

During the first week this often feels odd or performative. That feeling resolves as the body learns to let go. Stay with it.

04
Final 1 minute

Slow to stillness

Gradually decelerate the bouncing over the final minute until standing completely still. Take three slow, full breaths. Notice the difference in the body compared to one minute ago — the sense of internal vibration, warmth, looseness. That shift is the point. The transition into the flowing practice begins from here.

Part 2 — Slow Flowing Practice (10 minutes)

05
Transition — enter from stillness

Begin slow intentional movement

From the post-shaking stillness, begin slow, deliberate movement. There is no fixed routine required. The governing principle: slow, fluid arm and torso movement that generates genuine internal warmth and physical aliveness while the external appearance remains calm and controlled. Exhale on exertion, inhale on opening.

06
10 minutes — combine freely

Movement vocabulary to draw from

  • Slow arm circles — one arm at a time, then both; forward and backward
  • Torso rotation — arms trailing softly like wet cloth
  • Slow lateral side bend — one arm overhead, arc from side to side
  • Rising and sinking on slightly bent knees — like a gentle wave
  • Slow weight shift side to side, arms floating at waist height
  • Ba Duan Jin (Eight Brocades) — any movements from this sequence if familiar

Move at roughly half your normal pace. There is no wrong combination. The quality being cultivated: full internal presence in the body, coordinated breath, warmth inside — softness outside.

Continuity matters more than perfection. A consistent 15-minute practice done imperfectly every morning outperforms an occasional perfect session.

Minimum version (significant illness, fatigue, or time pressure): Shaking practice only — 3 minutes. Even this 3-minute version breaks the overnight bracing pattern and changes the nervous system's starting point for the day. Never skip entirely, even on difficult mornings.
Somatic Release

Somatic Release Protocol

Phase 1: 7–10 min morning only · Phase 3+: 10–15 min morning, 20–25 min pre-CC

🌱 Phase 1: Week 3 onward · Before Qi-Gong · Floor-based 🌱 Phase 3+: Full sequence each morning + extended pre-CC
Why this matters: Chronic hypervigilance creates sustained defensive bracing throughout the body — elevated shoulders, clenched jaw, contracted psoas, shallow chest breathing. This muscular tension signals ongoing danger to the brainstem, keeping the nervous system in anticipatory alert regardless of whether any trigger is currently present. The somatic release protocol directly interrupts this feedback loop from below. Body tension at CC session entry is one of the strongest predictors of session quality — the pre-CC sequence is not optional preparation; it is part of the intervention itself.
7–10 minutes · Every morning from Phase 1 Week 3. Run before the day's demands begin — before Qi-Gong, before the meeting. On very short mornings: the 7 minutes is enough. Do not skip it even when time is tight.
01
5 minutes

Constructive Rest Position

Lie on your back on the floor — not a soft mattress, which prevents the body from fully releasing. Knees bent, feet flat on the floor, hip-width apart. Hands resting on the lower belly. Close your eyes. Allow the lower back to release toward the floor — do not force it down, simply stop holding it up. Breathe into your hands: feel the belly rise on the inhale and fall on the exhale. Jaw unclenched, lips slightly parted. Eyes soft. Do not try to relax. Just stop efforting.

If the lower back will not release toward the floor: this is normal when tension is high. The releasing happens over minutes, not immediately. Stay with it.

02
2–3 minutes

Diaphragmatic Reset

Remaining in the same position, shift to counted breathing. Inhale slowly through the nose for 4 counts — belly rises, chest stays relatively still. Hold gently for 2 counts. Exhale slowly through the nose or mouth for 6 counts. The exhale is longer than the inhale — this directly activates the parasympathetic nervous system. On each exhale: allow the jaw to release slightly further, allow the shoulders to drop. Three to five cycles of this breathing should produce a clear, felt shift in tension level.

Morning: 10–15 minutes — all steps at a calm, unhurried pace. Pre-CC: 20–25 minutes — full sequence with more time in each step. The pre-CC version is mandatory on CC days. Body tension at session start is a direct predictor of outcome.
01
5 minutes

Constructive Rest Position

Lie on your back on the floor (not a mattress). Knees bent, feet flat, hip-width apart. Hands on lower belly. Allow the lower back to release toward the floor — stop holding it up rather than pressing it down. Breathe into the hands. Jaw released, eyes soft. The longer version: use a full 5 minutes here, noticing where in the body tension is still being held rather than trying to fix it.

02
5 minutes

Psoas Release

From constructive rest, allow one knee to slowly drop outward to the side — do not push it, let gravity take it. Allow it to travel only as far as it goes on its own without forcing. Hold 2 full minutes. Then slowly, over 30 seconds, bring it back to center. Repeat on the other side for 2 minutes. Final 30 seconds: both knees back to center, hands on belly.

What it should feel like: A gradual releasing sensation deep in the hip, lower back, or inner thigh — possibly spreading into the belly. Not a stretch. Not sharp pain. Release. Some sessions produce very little sensation; others produce significant release. Both are valid.

If it produces anxiety or sharp pain: Return both knees to center and remain in constructive rest for the remaining time. Do not force. The simplified version is always the right fallback.

03
3 minutes

Hip Circles

Stand up slowly, pausing if dizzy. Feet hip-width apart, hands resting on hips. Begin slow circles with the hips — as large and slow as is comfortable. One full minute circling in one direction. One full minute circling in the other direction. One minute of slow figure-eights. Move as slowly as possible. This is not exercise — it is allowing the hip joint to release accumulated compression from the floor work.

04
3 minutes

Shoulder and Neck Release

Head semicircles (1 minute): Slow semicircles forward only — ear toward one shoulder, chin toward chest, ear toward the other shoulder. Never roll the head backward. Move at one-quarter of normal speed. Let the weight of the head do the work.

Shoulder shrugs (1 minute): Raise both shoulders as high as possible toward the ears, hold 3 seconds, then drop them completely — not lower gradually, but release and drop. The drop should feel like letting go of something. Repeat slowly.

Jaw release (1 minute): Let the jaw drop open — do not control how far. Hold 5 seconds. Allow it to close. Repeat. The jaw holds an extraordinary amount of accumulated nervous system tension; this minute often produces more release than the physical work that preceded it.

05
3 minutes

Diaphragmatic Reset

Standing or sitting. Inhale 4 counts into the belly (belly expands outward, chest remains relatively still). Pause 2 counts. Exhale 6 counts with an audible, intentional release — a slow open-mouthed "haaah" is useful for the exhale. On each exhale: allow the jaw to drop, allow the shoulders to soften. Three cycles of this should produce a clear felt shift in baseline tension. Five cycles is the full version.

06
2 minutes

Integration

Stand normally. Do not try to change anything about how the body feels. Shake both hands loosely at the sides for 10 seconds — let the wrists go floppy. Take one slow, full breath from the belly. Scan the body from feet to the crown: where is tension still being held? You do not need to fix it. The act of noticing, without trying to change it, is itself a regulation intervention. The sequence is complete.

Do not force sharp pain in any position. The psoas release should feel like release, not stretch. If any position produces significant anxiety, return to constructive rest and stay there for the remaining time. The simplified Phase 1 version is always available as a fallback — on high-tension days it is often the right choice.
Regulation Practices
Regulation Practice · Daily

Resonance Frequency Breathing

6 breaths per minute · 20 minutes daily · Available anywhere, no device required

🕑 Morning: After Qi-Gong, before the meeting (Phase 1 Month 2+) 🕑 Pre-CC: Required entry condition before every CC session ✅ Also: Portable, usable in any context in real time
Why this matters: Six breaths per minute (5 seconds in, 5 seconds out) is the resonance frequency of the cardiovascular system — the rate at which breathing oscillations and heart rate oscillations synchronize into cardiac coherence. This synchronized state directly trains vagal tone, increases heart rate variability, and activates the prefrontal cortex's regulatory influence over the amygdala. This is the biological target of HeartMath biofeedback. The device teaches you to recognize the felt sense of this state; the practice then runs independently without it.
The critical distinction: The breathing pattern alone is not sufficient to produce cardiac coherence. A felt positive emotional state — physically felt in the body, not conceptually recalled — must be present simultaneously. Breathing at 6/min with neutral attention is just slow breathing. Breathing at 6/min while holding a genuinely felt positive state is the intervention.
01
Before beginning — 2 minutes

Find a genuinely felt positive state

Before the breathing begins, recall something that produces a warm, expansive, or genuinely positive physical feeling in the chest or belly — not just a positive thought, but a felt body sensation. Good sources: a memory of deep connection with someone, a moment of genuine awe or beauty, a place that reliably produces warmth or peace. The image or memory is less important than the physical feeling it generates. Rest in that feeling until it is present in the body.

If nothing comes: start with the breathing anyway. Often the felt state arrives once the breath is established. If the positive state genuinely cannot be found after 3 minutes, this is a Yellow day — do not attempt HeartMath or a CC session.

02
Full session · 20 minutes

The breathing pattern — 6 breaths per minute

Inhale through the nose for exactly 5 counts. Exhale through the nose or mouth for exactly 5 counts. No pause between. The inhalation begins the moment the exhalation ends. This produces exactly 6 complete breath cycles per minute.

Count method for beginners: count silently to yourself. 1-2-3-4-5 in. 1-2-3-4-5 out. After several sessions the rhythm becomes embodied and counting becomes unnecessary.

Maintain the felt positive state throughout. If attention drifts away from the felt state, gently return to it — the return is not a failure, it is the practice. Coherence comes and goes; the skill is noticing the shift and returning.

What coherence feels like when it is working: a distinct sense of settled aliveness in the chest, breathing feels slightly easier than the count suggests, time sense may shift slightly. Some people feel it immediately; others take several weeks of daily practice.

03
After the session

HeartMath integration (Month 2 onward)

If using the HeartMath Inner Balance device: clip the ear sensor to the left earlobe, open the app in Free Session (Quick Coherence) mode — never the guided sessions, as the narrator audio is a direct trigger. The app displays a real-time coherence score. The purpose is learning — understanding what coherence feels like in your specific nervous system by correlating the felt sense with the readout. Once you can reliably feel the shift without the device, the device becomes an occasional check-in rather than a daily requirement.

App note: Always Free Session mode. Always through headphones — never phone speaker. The guided audio programs contain mouth sounds.

Physiological Sigh — Real-Time Interrupt

→
Immediately after any trigger — 10–15 seconds

The fastest known manual autonomic reset

Double inhale through the nose: a full sniff in, then immediately a second sniff to top off the lungs. Follow with one long, complete, slow exhale through the mouth. The double inhale reinflates collapsed alveoli; the extended exhale activates the parasympathetic system. The effect is faster than any other volitional breathing technique. Use this in real-time after any trigger fires in daily life. It is not a substitute for the formal resonance frequency breathing practice — it is the immediate emergency tool.

Evening Practice · Non-Negotiable

Yoga Nidra

Every evening before sleep · Never skipped · Duration increases across phases

🕑 Timing: 30–60 min before sleep target · After tart cherry + bedtime supplements 🌞 Phases 1–2: 20 min · Phase 3: 30–35 min · Phase 4+: 40–45 min
Why this matters: Yoga Nidra produces a theta-alpha boundary state (brainwaves 4–12 Hz, just above sleep onset) in which the thalamus remains active as a sensory filter while the cortex enters low arousal. In this state, sounds register and pass without the cortical threat cascade being fully online. By practicing open awareness of ambient sounds — including trigger-range sounds — from within this state, the thalamic filter is trained directly over time. This is mechanistically distinct from resonance frequency breathing (vagal regulation from an alert state) and from counterconditioning (conscious association rewriting). Yoga Nidra trains what happens to sensory input in the passively filtered state.
This is the one practice that should never be skipped, even when everything else is missed. The night-waking cortisol loop that disrupts sleep architecture returns more quickly without Yoga Nidra than without any other practice in the protocol. On MDMA nights (1–3 nights after a session), when REM is disrupted and sleep is fragmented, Yoga Nidra maintains the theta-alpha state even if sleep does not return — which prevents the frustration loop from raising the following day's reactive baseline.
01
Setup — before pressing play

Environment and position

Lie on your back in bed or on the floor. If in bed, arrange pillows so the neck is neutral — not craned forward or back. A pillow under the knees reduces lower back tension and allows the legs to fully release. Eyes closed. Blackout mask on. Headphones in (over-ear preferred, or earbuds if side-sleeping). Pink noise or silence as background — not music.

Put the phone face-down where you can reach it for the track controls but cannot see the screen. Once the track begins, there is no checking the phone.

02
Track selection — do this work once, reuse it

Finding the right tracks

The track must include three phases: a body scan, a visualization phase, and an open awareness of external sounds phase. The open awareness phase is where the thalamic filter training happens — tracks that end before this phase provide relaxation but miss the therapeutic mechanism.

Starting points to test: Jennifer Piercy "Yoga Nidra for Sleep" (YouTube, ~27 min) · Huberman Lab NSDR (Non-Sleep Deep Rest, ~10 or 20 min) · Ally Boothroyd tracks (various lengths, Spotify or YouTube).

Test 3–4 tracks across 2 weeks. Identify the 2–3 that reliably produce the hypnagogic edge: a state where you are aware but deeply relaxed, where the body feels absent or heavy, and where time sense has shifted. These become your rotation.

The hypnagogic edge is the target state. Falling fully asleep during the track is not failure — it is a sign the nervous system needed it. Waking at the end is ideal but not required.

03
During — body scan phase

Following the body scan

Most tracks begin with a body scan — the guide directs attention to body parts sequentially. Follow it with soft, diffuse attention. Do not actively try to relax each body part; just bring attention there and allow whatever is present to be present. The goal is not to produce a specific sensation but to occupy attention in a way that prevents rumination from beginning.

04
During — open awareness phase

The most important phase: ambient sound awareness

When the track reaches the open awareness or external sounds phase, do not redirect attention away from what you hear — including sounds from the community space. Let them register. Label them internally if helpful: "footsteps," "voices," "wind," "eating." Allow each sound to pass without the body reacting. You are not suppressing the reaction — you are observing whether a reaction arises, from a state where the threat cascade is not fully engaged.

This is the core thalamic filter training. With consistent practice over weeks and months, sounds that previously activated the cascade begin to register and pass without escalation. The nervous system is being trained in its most permeable state.

If a high-trigger sound breaks the state: Note it. Return to the breath. Re-enter the body scan from wherever you were. The recovery — the return to stillness after disruption — is itself the training. It is more therapeutically valuable than an undisturbed session.

05
Night waking — specific protocol

If you wake during the night

Do not check the phone. Do not turn on any light. Do not look at the time. Reach for the phone and press play on the 20-minute sleep-specific Yoga Nidra track — the shortest one in your rotation. This maintains the theta-alpha state even if sleep does not fully return, and prevents the cortisol-driven frustration loop (awareness of not sleeping → rising anxiety → higher arousal → less sleep) that otherwise compounds through the night and raises the following day's reactive baseline.

The night waking protocol is one of the highest-leverage single practices in the protocol for protecting the next day's starting point. Its value comes specifically from using it immediately rather than lying awake first.

Track Length by Phase

Phases 1–220-minute tracks. Standard nights: sleep-specific. Intensive processing nights (post-CC, post-psilocybin integration): 35–45 min open awareness track.
Phase 330–35-minute tracks as the standard. The longer open awareness window is the progression.

Behavioral Rewriting
Counterconditioning · Phase 2 onward

Counterconditioning Techniques

6 techniques · Introduced in sequence · Each builds on the last · Green days only

🕑 Training days: Pre-Main Meal (11:30–2pm window) 🕑 Aerobic days: ~12:30pm (2h post-waterfall BDNF peak) 🔴 Green days only — never on Yellow or Red
The mechanism: The misophonic threat response fires via a conditioned circuit that activates faster than top-down regulation can reach it. Passive exposure to that cascade does not correct it — in many cases it deepens it. Counterconditioning is the operative mechanism: actively pairing the trigger sound with a genuinely felt positive emotional state, building a competing association strong enough to inhibit the threat response over time. Four conditions must be simultaneously present for new associations to write durably: a calm nervous system, genuine felt positive emotion, sufficient neuroplasticity, and sleep to consolidate afterward. This protocol is designed to produce all four, repeatedly.

Entry Conditions — Required Before Every Session

✓SUDs 0–2 — subjective units of distress, assessed right now, sitting quietly. If above 2: Yellow day. No session.
✓Body tension below 4/10 — scan from jaw to shoulders to belly. If above 4: complete the full somatic release sequence and reassess. Do not skip this.
✓Resonance frequency breathing established — 3–5 minutes minimum, felt positive state present. Not just calm: genuinely positive felt sensation in the body.

01 Neural Repatterning — Primary Technique Phase 2+ ►

What it is

The core technique. Pairing trigger sounds with genuine felt positive stimuli in a structured ratio (positive stimulus dominant, trigger introduced briefly). Every other technique is a modification of this one.

Full session sequence

Somatic releaseFull Phase 3 sequence, or simplified Phase 1 version. Body tension below 4/10 confirmed.
Resonance breathing3–5 minutes. SUDs 0–2 confirmed. Felt positive state present. Only proceed when both are true.
tVNS (if using)Nuropod clipped to left ear cymba concha. Start 2–3 minutes before the first repetition. Leave running throughout.
Positive primingBegin positive stimuli playlist (music, imagery, or memory that reliably produces warm felt sensation in the body). 2–3 minutes. Wait for the felt positive state — do not rush past this.
Introduce triggerIntroduce the trigger sound at low volume while positive stimuli continue. Positive music dominant (70% of perceived volume). Hold 2–4 minutes. The felt positive state must remain throughout. If it disappears, remove the trigger and return to positive priming.
Remove triggerRemove trigger sound. Return to positive stimuli only. Allow the positive state to rebuild fully — minimum 2 minutes. Do not rush this recovery interval.
Repeat4–6 repetitions per session total. Each cycle: priming → trigger → recovery. Same trigger or adjacent hierarchy level.
Close on positiveFinal 2 minutes: positive stimuli only, no trigger. Always end on positive. The last state of the session is what consolidates during sleep.
Log2 minutes immediately after: SUDs before session, SUDs after final rep, any observations. Brief and functional.

The 70/30 ratio

The positive stimulus must remain dominant throughout the trigger exposure. If the trigger volume or intensity is high enough to overwhelm the positive state, reduce the trigger. The ratio is not a metaphor — it is operational. Positive state present = competing association being written. Positive state absent = reinforcement of the original.

02 Graduated Volume Exposure Phase 2–3 ►

What it is

A systematic modification of Technique 1 that adds volume as a hierarchical dimension. Introduces the same trigger sound at progressively higher volumes across sessions — not within a single session.

How to apply it

Run the full Technique 1 protocol. The only addition: across sessions, track the volume at which the trigger produces SUDs 1–2. When two consecutive sessions at a given volume produce no more than a flicker of response, increase the volume by the smallest perceptible step at the following session.

Important: Volume is a full hierarchy dimension. The same chewing sound at slightly higher volume may rate 2–3 SUDs higher than at low volume. Do not underestimate this variable. Treat each volume increment as a new hierarchy level.

03 Interoceptive Awareness During Trigger Phase 3+ ►

What it is

A modification of Technique 1 that shifts the focus of attention during trigger exposure from the positive external stimuli to inward body sensation. Trains the shift from threat-scanning to interoception during the trigger response.

How to apply it

Run the full Technique 1 setup — somatic release, resonance breathing, entry conditions confirmed, positive priming. When the trigger is introduced, instead of directing attention toward the positive stimuli, turn attention inward: where in the body is the response registering? What quality does it have — hot or cold, tight or loose, sharp or diffuse? Is it moving or fixed? This is curiosity, not suppression.

Maintain the positive stimuli in the background throughout. The inward attention is a layer on top of the positive state, not a replacement for it.

Do not attempt this technique until Technique 1 is running smoothly — meaning 4–6 repetitions consistently producing post-session SUDs reduction across multiple sessions. The interoceptive shift requires a stable positive state underneath it.

04 Positive Body Anchor Phase 3+ ►

What it is

A physical anchor that maintains somatic connection to the positive state during trigger exposure. Pairs with Technique 1 or 3, particularly at higher hierarchy levels where the positive state becomes hard to hold.

How to apply it

Before the session begins, take 2–3 minutes to identify a specific body location that reliably holds the felt positive state — often the chest, the sternum, or the upper belly. Rest one hand gently on that location throughout the priming phase and confirm the felt state is present there.

During trigger exposure, keep the hand resting on that same location. The physical contact creates a somatic anchor — a direct body-based reminder of the positive state even when cognitive attention is occupied by the trigger or inward sensing. Particularly useful when working at higher hierarchy levels where the positive state requires active maintenance.

05 Compassion Imagery + Trigger Phase 3+ · Person-specific sounds ►

What it is

An extension of Technique 1 specifically for sounds whose charge is primarily relational — a specific person's eating sounds, breathing, chewing. Addresses the interpersonal-violation layer that standard CC cannot reach from the acoustic level alone.

How to apply it

Complete the full Technique 1 setup. Before introducing the trigger, spend 2–3 minutes cultivating a genuine sense of warmth toward the specific person. Not forgiveness — warmth. A concrete memory of genuine connection: something real between you. A moment of care they showed. Something you appreciated about them that you can actually feel. Allow that sense of warmth to be present in the body before any trigger is introduced.

Then introduce the trigger sound from that state of warmth. The positive stimulus is now not just music — it includes the felt warmth toward the triggering person specifically.

Not available until Phase 3. EMDR and MDMA work need to have already begun loosening the relational charge before this technique can operate. Attempting it before that groundwork is laid may produce flooding rather than conditioning. Do not force it.

06 Real-World Graduated Exposure Phase 4–5 ►

What it is

Moving the counterconditioning work from the laboratory of a formal session into a real-world context from the avoidance inventory. The formal session is designed; real-world graduated exposure is chosen. Both are required for full hierarchy resolution.

How to apply it

Choose one specific real-world context from the avoidance inventory — the one closest to the bottom of the list (lowest charge). Enter it with: bone conduction headphones at barely perceptible pink noise volume, resonance frequency breathing capability available, a clear and practiced exit plan, and the tVNS Nuropod if it helps. The target is a regulated experience in the real context — not flooding, not enduring. A short, complete, regulated exposure followed by a clean exit is success.

After the exposure, complete a brief post-session log: SUDs before entering, peak SUDs during, SUDs upon exit, time in context. Over multiple exposures in the same context, track whether the peak SUDs is declining.

The avoidance inventory is what ultimately matters as an outcome measure — not A-MISO-S scores alone. The shrinking of that list over the course of Phase 5 is the most concrete measure of protocol success.
